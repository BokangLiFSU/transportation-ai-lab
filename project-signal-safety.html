Research context

A traffic-control treatment may improve one outcome while leaving another unchanged. This research evaluates safety separately by crash type and severity, rather than inferring safety benefits from improved traffic operation.

Approach

The adaptive-signal study uses an observational before-and-after full-Bayes design with twenty treated and forty comparison intersections in Florida. It estimates crash modification factors for total, rear-end and severity-specific outcomes. Related studies use full-Bayes and empirical-Bayes approaches to examine pedestrian countdown signals and adaptive signal control.

The transit-priority study compares twelve treated corridors with twenty-nine comparison corridors. It finds statistically supported reductions in total and angle crashes, while the rear-end and sideswipe estimates remain uncertain at the reported interval. In the adaptive-signal study, the fatal-plus-injury estimate is not statistically significant at the stated 90% credible interval. These distinctions prevent an overall benefit from being generalized to every outcome.

Research contribution

  • Before-and-after evaluation with comparison sites and explicit uncertainty.
  • Crash modification factors distinguished by crash type and severity.
  • Separate evidence for pedestrian countdown signals, adaptive signals and transit priority.

This work provides a foundation in intervention evaluation and the interpretation of safety evidence. It informs questions about how emerging control technologies should be assessed against the people and crash outcomes they affect.
