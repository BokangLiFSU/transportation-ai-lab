Research context

An initial incident changes traffic conditions over space and time, creating additional crash risk. This research examines how secondary crashes should be identified and how traffic and incident data can inform a timely response.

Approach

The Florida’s Turnpike study combines incident records, high-resolution traffic measurements, roadway geometry and rainfall observations from January 2014 to June 2019. Its tasks include secondary-crash identification, associated-factor analysis, real-time likelihood estimation and exploration of connected-vehicle mitigation applications.

Related journal studies use Bayesian complementary log-log and penalized logistic models to estimate secondary-crash risk. A 2022 Turnpike study follows queue growth and dissipation at segment level. A comparison approach that assigned the same impact across affected segments identified 54% more secondary crashes; the difference concerns which events are classified, rather than a percentage improvement in prediction accuracy. The separate Jacksonville traffic-management-center evaluation uses hazard-based models to examine verification and response durations.

Published contributions

  • Methods for distinguishing secondary crashes using the evolving influence of a primary incident.
  • Risk models connecting incident, traffic, geometric and weather records.
  • Evaluation of response processes and service-patrol strategies, with original study settings retained.

This research makes event definition part of the modeling problem. A risk model can learn a different relationship when the boundary around an incident changes the crashes counted as secondary. Future learning-based analyses therefore need to examine label sensitivity and local traffic conditions before comparing prediction performance across corridors.
