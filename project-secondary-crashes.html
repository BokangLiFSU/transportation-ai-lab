Research question

An initial incident changes traffic conditions over space and time, creating additional crash risk. This research examines how secondary crashes should be identified and how traffic and incident data can inform a timely response.

Data and methods

The Florida Turnpike study combines incident records, high-resolution traffic measurements, roadway geometry and rainfall observations from January 2014 to June 2019. Its tasks include secondary-crash identification, associated-factor analysis, real-time likelihood estimation and exploration of connected-vehicle mitigation applications.

Related journal studies estimate secondary-crash risk using Bayesian complementary log-log and penalized logistic models. The 2022 Turnpike study represents queue growth and dissipation separately for affected segments. Its comparison measures changes in crash classification, not a percentage improvement in prediction accuracy. A separate Jacksonville evaluation uses hazard-based models to analyze incident verification and response durations.

Published contributions

  • The comparison approach identified 54% more secondary crashes than the segment-specific queue method. The additional cases tended to occur under lighter congestion.
  • Risk models relate secondary-crash likelihood to incident characteristics and prevailing operating conditions.
  • The 2021 Turnpike report combines event identification, associated-factor analysis, real-time prediction and exploration of connected-vehicle mitigation applications.

Investigators and collaborators

The 2021 Turnpike report was authored by Priyanka Alluri, Thobias Sando, Angela Kitali, Denis Monyo and Haifeng Wang. Earlier Jacksonville research involved UNF and FIU investigators. The linked publications identify the separate author teams.
